# Weekend Market Note — Week of Mar 31–Apr 4, 2026

The dominant theme this week was the shift from a pure oil-price shock to a broader macro constraint story. Markets are increasingly pricing not only higher energy costs, but also the fact that governments and central banks have much less room than in past crises to cushion the fallout.

Energy

The energy shock became more structural. Saudi crude pricing moved to record premiums, highlighting how restricted Gulf supply has become even when benchmark prices fluctuate. At the same time, traders struggled to monetize volatility because the disruption is no longer just about price swings, but about physical insecurity, stranded cargoes, insurance costs and broken logistics.

A second important shift is that the market is starting to think beyond the immediate crisis. Gulf states are revisiting pipeline and export bypass routes, which shows the Strait of Hormuz is no longer being treated as a temporary bottleneck but as a strategic vulnerability that may reshape regional energy infrastructure.

Rates

Rates remain difficult to interpret through the old playbook. The issue is no longer just inflation expectations, but rising term premium driven by fiscal stress. Bond markets are signaling concern that governments, already running large deficits and debt loads, may still be forced into subsidies, rationing support and extra spending despite having little policy space left.

This makes the shock more dangerous than previous energy spikes: slower growth would normally support bonds, but fiscal fragility is keeping duration less defensive than usual.

Equities and volatility

Equity markets stayed fragile as investors continued to face a stagflationary mix of weaker growth and higher energy costs. Volatility remains elevated because markets are trying to price both physical supply disruption and macro deterioration at the same time.

A key insight from the week is that even sophisticated commodity traders struggled early in the crisis, which underlines how disorderly and operationally complex the environment has become.

FX

FX remained secondary to the dominant oil and rates narrative, but the broader message is clear: countries with high energy-import dependence and weak fiscal space remain the most exposed. Richer economies may outbid others for supply, but that still comes with slower growth and tighter policy trade-offs.

What to watch next week

Focus will remain on whether Hormuz disruption becomes more entrenched and whether Gulf exporters can keep redirecting enough volumes through alternative routes. Markets will also watch whether fiscal strain starts to matter more explicitly in bond pricing, especially in high-deficit economies.

The key risk is that the shock spreads further from energy into sovereign funding stress and demand destruction, turning an oil shock into a wider macro regime shift.
