# Weekend Macro Brief — March 21 2026

Driver  
Markets moved deeper into an energy-shock regime this week as the Iran conflict stretched into a third week and investors increasingly treated higher oil and gas prices as persistent rather than temporary. The key shift was from pricing delayed rate cuts to actively discussing the possibility of renewed tightening, especially in front-end rates and in the UK.

Macro Developments  
The main macro development this week was the transmission of the energy shock into bond markets and central bank pricing. In the US, traders moved from expecting cuts to assigning meaningful odds to a Fed hike later this year as higher oil prices lifted inflation expectations and pushed short-term Treasury yields sharply higher. In Europe and the UK, the shock was even more visible: the BoE’s hawkish tone triggered a violent sell-off in gilts and reinforced fears that a commodity shock could morph into a broader bond and fiscal shock. The market narrative is no longer just about disrupted oil flows, but about whether central banks can tolerate another inflation wave without tightening financial conditions further.

Transmission  

Energy: Oil stayed elevated near stress levels, with US oil ending around 98 and the market still focused on supply disruption through Hormuz and broader Gulf energy infrastructure risk.

Rates: Government bonds sold off heavily, with US 2Y at 3.91 and US 10Y at 4.38 by week-end, showing that inflation risk and higher-for-longer policy pricing are now feeding through the whole curve.

FX: The dollar strengthened as higher US yields and global stress supported USD demand. USDJPY and USDCNH both moved higher, while EURUSD softened.

Equities: Equities turned more defensive, with the S&P 500 down about 1.5% on the day and the VIX back near 27, reflecting a clearer risk-off tone as higher yields and stagflation fears pressured valuations.

Market Narrative  
The week marked a transition from an “energy shock with temporary policy uncertainty” to a more serious stagflation discussion. Oil is no longer just a geopolitical variable; it is now shaping rate expectations, fiscal stress and cross-asset positioning. The US remains relatively more insulated than Europe as an energy producer, but even there the market is starting to question whether the Fed can cut at all if energy inflation remains elevated. In the UK, the gilt sell-off showed how quickly an oil shock can become a duration and refinancing problem for governments with limited fiscal room.

Key Points to Watch  
• Whether oil breaks and holds above the 100 level again  
• Any sign that the Fed hike pricing proves excessive or starts to look more realistic  
• Whether long-end yields continue to rise alongside the front end, deepening stagflation fears  
• Further stress in UK and European bond markets after the BoE communication shock  
• Dollar strength versus energy-importing economies and vulnerable currencies
